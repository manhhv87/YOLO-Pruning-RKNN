\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports what two real cabbage-field robot runs establish, separating
what those runs \emph{measure} from what still depends on a larger or independent
labelling effort. The pipeline under test is the detector-free and training-free
guidance stack of Section~\ref{sec:method}: an Excess-Green
index~\cite{woebbecke1995,meyer2008} column-peak central-row tracker restricted to
a fixed ground safety corridor, a robust quadratic fit $x=P(y)$ by Huber
IRLS~\cite{huber1964}, a look-ahead tangent that defines the heading $\theta$
together with the cross-track offset $e$, and a temporal stage of a sliding-window
median, an exponential moving average, and a physical max-jump gate applied to
both signals. The system is never trained, so the two runs serve as a pure test
set; no parameters are fitted on these frames, which removes any path for leakage.

One distinction governs the numbers that follow. Self-consistency is not
correctness: the row-found rate of Section~\ref{sec:res-reliability} records only
that the tracker \emph{emitted} a guidance line, and it carries no claim about
whether that line is the correct central row. Likewise, the post-fusion jump
counts of Section~\ref{sec:res-temporal} fall to near zero as a direct effect of
the gate threshold and are reported as descriptive context, not as a robustness
result. The correctness claim is settled separately in
Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy} as error against a human labelling of the central
row, including on the frames the gate suppresses. Cross-track is reported in
pixels, as a percentage of the half-image width, and in centimetres through the
image-to-ground homography of Section~\ref{sec:method:platform}, built from the
robot's measured camera height and tilt.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:res-dataset}
% =====================================================================

The evaluation data are two forward-view video runs recorded by the onboard camera
of a cabbage field robot, sampled at $1$\,fps and screened to $1482$ forward-view
cabbage still frames at $640\times480$. The
two runs cover different sections of the field at different times of day, so
vegetation density and lighting differ between them. Table~\ref{tab:res-dataset}
summarises the runs. The setting is one robot, one crop, and one camera pose, and
the dataset supports no claim of coverage beyond this narrow operating envelope.

\subsection{Row-emission reliability}
\label{sec:res-reliability}
% =====================================================================

We first assess whether the perception front end emits a guidance line on every
frame. Across all $1482$ frames the Excess-Green column-peak tracker emitted a
guidance line in $99.9\%$ of frames ($1481/1482$); a single frame produced no
peak inside the seed corridor and was abstained on. To test whether this figure
conceals a silent failure mode in sparse or dense canopy, we split the frames into
vegetation tertiles by per-frame Excess-Green coverage (low, mid, high).
Table~\ref{tab:res-reliability} shows the emit rate is $100\%$ in the low and mid
tertiles and $99.8\%$ in the high tertile. This stratification is partly
tautological by construction: the tracker locates the strongest Excess-Green
column within the safety corridor, so a peak almost always exists whenever any
green is present. The result therefore shows only that the tracker essentially
never aborts; whether the canopy density leaves the emitted line on the correct
row is the separate accuracy question of Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy}.

Mapping the fused near-row pixel offset through the image-to-ground homography
(Section~\ref{sec:method:platform}) describes the magnitude of the lateral offset
in metric units. After the temporal stage the central row sat a median of
$1.7$\,cm from the image centre across the runs (mean $3.4$\,cm, 90th percentile
$10.8$\,cm). These figures characterise the \emph{magnitude} of the lateral offset
along the recorded trajectory; they summarise where the row sat in the frame rather
than the accuracy of the proposed estimator, which is reported against ground truth
in Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy}.

\subsection{Temporal stability}
\label{sec:res-temporal}
% =====================================================================

The temporal stage, a sliding-window median followed by an exponential moving
average and a physical max-jump gate in the spirit of standard recursive
smoothing~\cite{kalman1960}, exists to suppress single-frame excursions that a
controller should not act on. Table~\ref{tab:res-temporal} reports how many
per-frame heading jumps exceed $10^\circ$, $15^\circ$, and $20^\circ$ in the raw
per-frame tracker output and after the temporal stage. The per-frame jump is the
change in heading from the previous frame and is undefined for the first frame of
each run, so the denominators are the $810$ and $669$ logged transitions.

These counts are descriptive, since the gate suppresses the jumps by design: the
post-fusion counts fall to near zero because the max-jump gate rejects exactly
these large frame-to-frame changes, which is an arithmetic property of the gate
threshold rather than an independent measurement of correctness. Whether the
temporal stage actually helps, namely whether the fused estimate is closer to
human ground truth than the raw estimate on the suppressed frames and how
selectively the gate fires, is the ablation reported in
Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy}.

\subsection{Accuracy and ablation evaluation}
\label{sec:res-accuracy}
\label{sec:res-baselines}
% =====================================================================

The quantities on which the deployment claim rests are the accuracy against a
human labelling of the central row and the comparisons that interpret it. A subset
of $150$ frames was hand-labelled by one annotator as a representative sample,
stratified across the two runs and the vegetation tertiles with even temporal
spacing. Because the raw front end produces large heading excursions on a sizeable
fraction of frames, this representative subset already contains $62$ such frames,
enough to test the temporal ablation without enriching the sample for difficulty. For
each labelled frame the annotator marks the central navigation row, and the
ground-truth (GT) line and its look-ahead tangent are derived in the same
$x=P(y)$ parameterisation as the estimator. Because the labelling was performed by
a single annotator, an inter-annotator agreement floor on the smallest meaningful
error is not available, and establishing one is left to future work; the errors
below are therefore read against a single human reference.

\paragraph{Accuracy against ground truth.}
Table~\ref{tab:res-accuracy} reports the heading and cross-track error of the
estimator against the labelled subset, for the raw per-frame output and for the
fused output after the temporal stage. The fused heading error has a median
of $5.0^\circ$ (mean $6.3^\circ$, 90th percentile $13.2^\circ$), and three
quarters of the labelled frames fall within $10^\circ$. The fused cross-track
error has a median of $10$\,px, which is about $1$\,cm through the calibrated
homography. The error distribution has a heavy tail: a minority of frames in which
the Excess-Green front end locks onto a neighbouring row rather than the central
one raise the cross-track mean to $18$\,px and the 90th percentile to $43$\,px
($\approx4.6$\,cm). These are the cases in which a vegetation index, which has no notion
of which plants belong to the crop, is least reliable, and the trained-detector
baseline below confirms they are where a learned detector helps most.

\paragraph{Temporal-stage ablation.}
The temporal stage improves accuracy, and not only smoothness. On the $62$
labelled frames on which the max-jump gate fired, the raw heading error has a
median of $11.8^\circ$ against ground truth, which the temporal stage reduces to
$8.1^\circ$; the effect replicates across both field runs taken separately (gated
heading error $12.7^\circ\!\to\!8.0^\circ$ and $11.8^\circ\!\to\!8.3^\circ$). The
gate is also selective, in that the mean raw heading error is
$14.4^\circ$ on the gated frames against $5.1^\circ$ on the rest, so the gate fires
on genuinely poor frames rather than rejecting good ones. This is the
non-circular form of the temporal result: unlike the jump counts of
Section~\ref{sec:res-temporal}, the comparison is against an external reference on
exactly the frames the stage acts upon. The two front-end and temporal additions
that target the wrong-row tail are consistent with this picture. The seed-corridor
width is the one perception hyperparameter selected with reference to this labelled
subset: sweeping it, the large cross-track errors ($>100$\,px) fall from $23$ (no
corridor) to $18$ ($\pm220$\,mm) to $13$ ($\pm180$\,mm), while $\pm150$\,mm
over-tightens and worsens the cross-track tail, so $\pm180$\,mm is adopted; because
this touches the evaluation subset, choosing it on a disjoint set is left to future
work. Stabilising the cross-track signal through the temporal stage, rather than
reporting it per frame, then reduced its 90th-percentile error from $74$\,px (raw)
to $43$\,px (fused).

\paragraph{Fit-arm comparison.}
Replacing the pipeline's Huber IRLS fit with the two standard alternatives on the
same anchors, each passed through the same fit wrapper so that only the loss
differs, changes the result little (Table~\ref{tab:res-fitarm}). The Huber and
equal-weight least-squares fits are statistically indistinguishable: the paired
per-frame difference has a median of $0.0^\circ$ in heading and $0.0$\,px in
cross-track, each fit is the lower of the two on about a third of frames, and the
small gaps in the aggregate mean and tail are not a systematic advantage of
either. RANSAC is the weakest of the three ($7.2^\circ$ median heading against
$6.3^\circ$), because its hard inlier threshold discards anchors the other fits
use. On these corridor-restricted anchors the fit choice is therefore immaterial
within the least-squares family, which is consistent with the temporal stage
rather than the fit being the robustness lever; the Huber fit matches
equal-weight least squares here while retaining robustness against outlier anchors
when they occur.

\paragraph{Trained-detector baseline.}
A trained YOLOv5 cabbage detector provides the comparison baseline: its detections are filtered by a
$\pm220$\,mm safety corridor and the same curve is fit to them (Table~\ref{tab:res-detector}), so the two
methods share the downstream fit and differ in their perception front end. The headline comparison is between
the two methods as each is constituted: the complete proposed pipeline, with its temporal stage, against the
trained detector. The trained detector has the lower point estimate on every aggregate---heading median $4.2^\circ$ against
$5.0^\circ$, mean $6.1$ against $6.3$, 90th percentile $11.9$ against $13.2$, and a uniformly lower
cross-track---and it is decisively better on the wrong-row tail. The per-frame paired difference is, however,
small relative to the sample's resolution: a median of $0.2^\circ$ with a run-clustered $95\%$ bootstrap
interval of $[-0.5, 1.2]^\circ$ (cross-track $1.1$\,px, $[-0.9, 3.1]$). An advantage of up to about
$1.2^\circ$ therefore cannot be excluded at $n=150$; the gap is not resolvable here rather than demonstrably
zero. The detector's decisive advantage is on the $13$ frames where the Excess-Green tracker locks onto a
neighbouring row: there it localises the central row to a median cross-track error of $13$\,px against the
raw tracker's $201$\,px, better on every one of those frames. Two checks bound the comparison. Giving the
detector the identical temporal stage, reported as an ablation, barely moves it ($4.2^\circ\!\to\!4.1^\circ$,
cross-track 90th percentile unchanged at $32$\,px) and the matched paired difference is still unresolved
($0.5^\circ$, $[-0.4, 1.2]$), so the small gap does not rest on smoothing one side and not the other. And
stratifying by how far the ground-truth row sits off-centre shows the gap does not widen into the most
off-centre tercile ($|e^{\mathrm{GT}}|>39$\,px: $6.9^\circ$ proposed against $5.6^\circ$ detector). The
detector-free pipeline thus comes within the sample's resolution of the trained detector in the typical case,
at no labelling or GPU cost, while remaining behind it in point estimate and on the worst-case wrong-row
frames.

\subsection{Front-end, index, and temporal baselines}
\label{sec:res-frontend}
% =====================================================================

The fit-arm comparison varied the curve fit; we further varied the perception
front end, the vegetation index, and the temporal filter, each through the same
downstream and scored on the labelled subset (Table~\ref{tab:res-frontend}). Two
patterns emerge. First, the temporal stage lowers the fused heading error below
the raw per-frame error for every usable front end and index: the ExG tracker
improves from $6.3^\circ$ to $5.0^\circ$, ExGR from $6.0^\circ$ to $4.0^\circ$,
CIVE from $5.7^\circ$ to $5.0^\circ$, and the centroid-per-band tracker from
$7.8^\circ$ to $5.2^\circ$, so the improvement is a property of the temporal stage
rather than of any one front end. Second, the column-peak trackers cluster at a
fused median of $4$--$5^\circ$ whichever standard index feeds them, so the index
choice is immaterial: the sub-degree differences among the standard indices (ExGR
nominally lowest at $4.0^\circ$, ExG and CIVE at $5.0^\circ$) sit within the paired
bootstrap interval and are not ranked. The two
genuinely different front ends fare worse: the periodic-row tracker reaches
$8.4^\circ$, and the Hough-line detector is the clear outlier, emitting a guidance
line on only $59\%$ of frames and reaching $23.9^\circ$, because the discrete,
gapped cabbage plants do not form the continuous edges a line detector relies on.
The temporal stage does not rescue Hough -- its fused error is no lower than its
raw error -- because its failures are systematic rather than the transient spikes
the median and gate are designed to remove. Across the usable front ends and
indices the temporal stage is therefore what lowers the error, and the perception
choice is secondary.

The temporal stage is also preferable to a more elaborate filter. Replacing the
proposed median, exponential moving average, and jump gate with a
constant-velocity Kalman filter on the same raw ExG heading stream gives a fused
median heading error of $5.8^\circ$, between the raw $6.3^\circ$ and the proposed
$5.0^\circ$; the simple stage, whose complexity is matched to the $1$\,fps regime,
is therefore sufficient and marginally better here.

The two principal limitations of these accuracy figures are the single annotator,
for which an inter-annotator agreement floor is left to future work, and the
residual wrong-row tail of the vegetation-index front end that the detector
comparison identifies as the main accuracy gap.
