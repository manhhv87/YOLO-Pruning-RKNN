\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\noindent Autonomous ground robots are increasingly used for the between-row work in precision agriculture
that once required manual scouting and hand-steered passes. Their core requirement is to remain centred on
the crop row across varying canopy and growth-stage conditions. A forward-view camera observes the canopy, a perception module localises the
central row, and a \emph{guidance line} summarising where the row leads is passed to a steering controller
that keeps the vehicle centred~\cite{cth1,cth2,cth6}. On a low-cost robot without RTK-GPS this vision-only
guidance line is the sole interface between perception and actuation, and what the controller consumes is
a \emph{heading} and a \emph{cross-track offset}, not a detection score~\cite{cth3,sogaard}.

\noindent Most crop-row systems obtain that line from a trained object detector, which raises two
difficulties. The detector is optimised for a box-level objective such as mean average precision, not for the
heading the controller consumes. It also
needs field-specific labelled data that is costly to obtain and prone to leakage between training and
test, and it introduces a learned failure surface that is hard to audit in the field~\cite{cth1,sogaard}.
We therefore investigate whether crop-row guidance can be achieved without a trained detector.

\noindent We study a deliberately simple pipeline assembled entirely from classical computer vision and
evaluate it in the field on a cabbage robot. An Excess-Green vegetation index~\cite{woebbecke1995,meyer2008}
and a column-peak tracker locate the central row; a robust quadratic fit $x=P(y)$ models its
curvature~\cite{huber1964,marchant1995}; the heading is taken as the look-ahead tangent to the fitted curve
and the cross-track as its lateral offset; and a temporal stage of a sliding-window median, an exponential
moving average, and a physical jump gate stabilises both signals over time~\cite{kalman1960}. The pipeline
has no learned weights and trains on nothing, so there is no train/test split to leak and the same code
runs unchanged across crops and growth stages. All components are established; our aim is to quantify the
guidance accuracy and temporal stability this stock composition achieves in field operation.

\noindent This paper makes three contributions. \emph{(i)}~A public forward-view cabbage-robot crop-row
guidance dataset, recorded on a working field robot at a camera pose representative of low-cost in-row
navigation. \emph{(ii)}~A leakage-free in-field evaluation of the detector-free pipeline against a
human-labelled subset, including a comparison with a trained YOLOv5 detector baseline: the complete pipeline
reaches per-frame parity in median heading (paired difference $0.2^\circ$, $95\%$ bootstrap interval
$[-0.5,1.2]$ spanning zero) at zero labelling and GPU cost---a parity that persists when the detector is
given the same temporal stage---while the trained detector is ahead only in the error tail and on the
wrong-row frames, the measured cost of going detector-free. \emph{(iii)}~The finding that, across the front ends and indices tested, the temporal stage
rather than the perception choice is the main source of heading stability.

\noindent The scope is deliberately narrow. We study one robot, one crop, and one camera pose, and we make
no claim of generalisation beyond this setting.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the platform, the pipeline, and the evaluation protocol;
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results; and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion}
discuss the findings and conclude.
