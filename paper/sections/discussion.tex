\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This is an applied contribution: an in-field evaluation of a detector-free, training-free guidance pipeline
of stock parts on a cabbage robot, not a new method. The discussion takes up why the temporal stage rather
than the perception front end carries the robustness, the failure modes of the vegetation-index front end
and the cost of avoiding a trained detector, and the limits of the evidence.

\subsection{Why the temporal stage is the robustness lever}
\label{sec:disc-safety}
A steering controller integrates whatever heading it is handed, so what matters is not the mean heading
error but the rate of \emph{large} errors: one frame in which the Excess-Green front end latches onto a
weed, a gap, or the wrong row can swing the look-ahead tangent by tens of degrees, and such a spike, if
acted on, could drive the robot into the crop. Suppressing such spikes is the temporal stage's job; the
raw-versus-fused jump counts of Table~\ref{tab:res-temporal} confirm that the gate does so by construction,
and the test that matters is error against ground truth on the frames it acts upon.

The Excess-Green tracker is a single-frame estimator that commits, per frame, to the strongest column of
vegetation contrast with no memory of the last few seconds; any stock single-frame front end shares this
weakness, because the errors come from scene content (a weed band, a canopy gap, an adjacent row) rather
than from the estimator. The temporal stage -- a median that rejects isolated contaminated frames, an EMA
that limits how fast the target moves, and a jump gate at the vehicle's kinematic limit -- is what converts
an occasionally-wrong per-frame estimate into a heading a controller can integrate. The labelled ablation
bears this out: on the gate-suppressed frames it lowers the median heading error against GT from
$11.8^{\circ}$ to $8.1^{\circ}$, and the gate fires selectively, with a mean raw error of $14.4^{\circ}$ on
the frames it holds against $5.1^{\circ}$ on the rest (Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy}). The fit-arm and
front-end/index baselines point the same way: the fit choice is secondary and the temporal stage improves
every front end and index tried, so within the set we tested the temporal stage, not the perception choice,
is the lever, although generality beyond that set is unproven. One caveat is unmeasured: the gate's
selectivity shows the frames it holds carry larger errors on average, but whether it ever vetoes a
\emph{correct} sharp correction -- at a headland, a bend, or a genuine drift -- is the false-reject rate
that a dedicated trial with annotated sharp-correction events would need to establish before the gate can be
called a safety mechanism.

\subsection{Failure modes of the Excess-Green front end}
\label{sec:disc-exg}
Because the temporal stage can only stabilise what the front end gives it, the
failure modes of the Excess-Green tracker bound the system. They are the familiar
limits of vegetation-index row
sensing~\cite{woebbecke1995,meyer2008,marchant1995}, and they bear on cabbage
forward-view imagery in specific ways.
\emph{Low vegetation contrast:} the Excess-Green index separates green canopy
from soil by colour, so at early growth stages, over wet or dark soil that is
itself greenish, or under flat overcast light, the column-peak signal is weak and
the tracked centre wanders.
\emph{Weeds:} inter-row weeds are also green and also produce Excess-Green
columns, so a weed band between rows can out-peak the crop row and pull the
tracker off-centre, a content failure that no amount of robust fitting on a
single frame can detect.
\emph{Canopy closure:} as cabbage matures and neighbouring rows touch, the
inter-row soil gap that defines the column structure closes, the peaks merge, and
the central-row column becomes ambiguous; the tracker may then follow the seam
between two rows rather than a row centre.
\emph{Gaps and missing plants:} a stretch of missing plants removes vegetation
contrast locally, so the look-ahead portion of the curve is fit on little or no
support and the tangent becomes unstable exactly where heading is most sensitive.
In all four cases the front end produces a confident-looking but wrong per-frame
line, which is why the temporal stage matters. It is also why the temporal
stage's protection is bounded by its false-reject rate: a sustained content
failure such as a long weed band or a closed-canopy stretch is not a transient
spike, and a median-plus-gate designed for spikes will eventually track it. The ground safety corridor and
the temporal stabilisation of the cross-track reduce this wrong-row tail but do not remove it; the residual
is the content failure a vegetation index cannot resolve, and it is exactly where the trained detector is
decisively more accurate (Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy}).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several boundaries limit how far these conclusions generalise. This study measures
guidance accuracy against ground truth; it does not close the steering loop. The
smoothness of the fused heading trace is an accuracy property and not a measured
closed-loop tracking result, and the temporal parameters (window length, EMA
factor, jump threshold) interact with controller and vehicle dynamics that an
accuracy evaluation does not exercise. Integrating the guidance into the steering
loop and measuring the realised intervention rate and tracking error with wheel
odometry is the clear next step. The accuracy itself is, however, robust to how
far the row sits off-centre: stratifying every trained-detector comparison by
$|e^{\mathrm{GT}}|$ shows the pipeline-versus-detector parity persists into the
most off-centre tercile (Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy}).

The metric figures rest on a measured camera pose and a flat-ground assumption. The heading $\theta$
is reported in degrees, which is controller-relevant and scale-free, and the
cross-track offset $e$ is reported in pixels, as a fraction of half the image
width, and in centimetres. The centimetre conversion uses an image-to-ground
homography built from the robot's own measured camera height ($65$\,cm) and tilt
($55^{\circ}$ from the vertical), with the camera intrinsics reused from a
checkerboard calibration of the same camera. A flat-ground homography still
inflates metric error most at the far look-ahead distance, precisely where
guidance is most sensitive, so the centimetre figures away from the near row
carry that planar-ground caveat. A full on-robot checkerboard calibration,
including the intrinsics, would further tighten the metric mapping.

The scope is narrow: two runs, one crop, one camera pose. The evidence is two
real cabbage runs ($\sim 20$ and $\sim 14$ minutes, $1482$ curated cabbage frames at $1$\,fps,
$640\times 480$), from one robot, one crop, and one forward-view camera pose.
This is a pure test set, since there is no training stage anywhere in the
pipeline and nothing can leak from these frames into the method, but it is also a
small, single-condition sample. With $n=2$ runs the across-field, across-season,
and across-platform variability cannot be characterised, and the descriptive
front end statistics already differ markedly between the two runs (raw
$>10^\circ$ jumps in $14.1\%$ versus $28.3\%$ of transitions, that is $114/810$ and
$189/669$), which is itself a caution against generalising from two clips.

Finally, accuracy is measured against a single annotator, so an inter-annotator
agreement floor on the smallest meaningful error is not established; a second
annotator is left to future work. To avoid over-reading differences that may sit
within label noise, comparisons whose paired bootstrap interval spans zero -- including the sub-degree
gap between the detector-free pipeline and the trained detector, and the Huber-versus-least-squares fit
difference -- are reported as statistically indistinguishable rather than ranked.
